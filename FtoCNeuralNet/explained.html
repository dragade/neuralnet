A neural network, explained

How a computer learns to turn Fahrenheit into Celsius without being told how

We'll build the world's smallest "brain": one artificial neuron. We show it a few examples, and it figures out the conversion on its own. By the end you'll understand the main idea behind every neural network, including the giant ones behind AI chatbots.

1The puzzle

The US measures temperature in Fahrenheit (°F). Most of the world uses Celsius (°C). Water freezes at 32°F, which is 0°C. It boils at 212°F, which is 100°C.

There's a formula to convert between them, and you may have seen it in math class:

C = (F − 32) × 5 ÷ 9

Here's the challenge: what if we didn't know the formula? All we have is a list of temperatures that someone measured both ways:

Can a computer look at these 7 examples and work out the rule by itself? That's what a neural network does: it learns from examples instead of being given the rules. These examples are called the training data.

2Meet the neuron: a machine with two knobs

Our neural network is just one neuron. Think of it as a tiny machine. A number goes in, the machine does two things to it, and a number comes out:

guess = multiply knob × F + add knob

Scientists have official names for the knobs. The multiply knob is called the weight (written w), and the add knob is called the bias (written b). So you'll often see:

guess = w × F + b

Here's the key idea: everything the neuron "knows" is stored in those two knobs. At the start, both are set to 0, so the neuron knows nothing. It guesses 0°C for every temperature. Learning means finding the right knob settings.

3Your turn: be the neuron

Before the computer tries, you try. Drag the two knobs below. The orange dots are the 7 correct answers. The blue line shows what the neuron would guess for every temperature. Your goal is to get the line to pass through all the dots.

Notice: the multiply knob changes how steep the line is (tilting it), and the add knob slides the whole line up or down. You need both to get it right.

4Keeping score: how wrong is it?

To get better, the neuron needs to know how badly it's doing. So we give it a mistake score. A big score means very wrong, and a score of 0 means perfect. Scientists call this the loss.

Here's how the score is calculated:

  1. For each example, find the miss: the neuron's guess minus the right answer. These are the little vertical bars on the chart above.
  2. Square each miss (multiply it by itself).
  3. Take the average of all the squared misses.

Example: the very first guess (both knobs at 0)

The neuron guesses 0°C for everything. For 212°F, the right answer is 100°C, so it misses by 100. Squared, that's 100 × 100 = 10,000. For 32°F, it guesses 0°C, which happens to be exactly right, so the miss is 0. Do this for all 7 examples and average them, and you get a score of about 1,991. That's terrible! There's lots of room to improve.

Why square the misses? Two reasons. First, some misses are too high (positive) and some are too low (negative). If we just added them up, they could cancel out and hide the mistakes. Squaring makes every miss positive. Second, squaring makes big mistakes count a lot more than small ones: a miss of 10 scores 100, but a miss of 20 scores 400. The neuron cares most about fixing its worst mistakes.

5How the computer turns the knobs

You probably fixed the line by eye. A computer can't "see" the chart, so it needs a different strategy. Here's the trick it uses.

Imagine you're standing on a hillside in thick fog. You can't see anything, but you want to reach the bottom of the valley. What do you do? Feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, then take a step downhill. Repeat this over and over, and you'll end up at the bottom.

The steps get smaller near the bottom because the ground gets flatter there, so the neuron slows down as it gets close.

For our neuron, the "hillside" is the mistake score. Every possible knob setting has a score, and the computer wants the setting with the lowest one. For each knob, it asks: "If I turned this knob up a tiny bit, would the score go up or down?" That direction is called the gradient (another word for slope). Then it turns each knob a small amount in the direction that makes the score go down.

Each check-and-adjust is one round of learning, called an epoch. Here are the four steps in every round:

1
GuessRun all 7 temperatures through the neuron.
2
ScoreWork out the mistake score.
3
Find downhillWork out which way each knob should turn.
4
Turn the knobsMove each one a small step that way.

Then repeat, again and again. This method is called gradient descent: "descent" means going down, and "gradient" means the slope.

How big should each step be?

The size of each step is called the learning rate, and choosing it matters:

  • Too small: the neuron is careful but painfully slow. It might need thousands of rounds.
  • Just right: it gets to the bottom quickly.
  • Too big: it jumps right over the bottom of the valley and lands higher up on the other side. Then it jumps back even further, and things get worse and worse. Its guesses "blow up."

Now let the computer do it. Pick a step size and press Start learning. The knobs start at 0 and the computer turns them itself, using the four steps above. Watch the line swing into place and the score drop. Then try the other step sizes!

So after 200 rounds, the neuron's rule is:

guess = 0.5556 × F − 17.78

Now compare that to the real formula. With a little algebra, you can rewrite it the same way:

C = (F − 32) × 5⁄9  =  5⁄9 × F − 32 × 5⁄9  =  0.5556 × F − 17.78

It's the same formula! The neuron discovered that you multiply by 5/9 (0.5556) and subtract 17.78. Nobody told it. It found them just by shrinking its mistakes, one small step at a time.

Did it really learn, or just memorize?

A good test is to try temperatures the neuron never saw during training. If it only memorized the 7 examples, it would get these wrong.

All correct. It learned the rule, not just the examples. That's the whole point of machine learning: get good at examples you haven't seen before.

8One small trick: shrinking the numbers

If you read the actual program, you'll notice one extra step: before the temperature goes into the neuron, it's divided by 100. So 212°F goes in as 2.12.

Why? When the computer works out which way to turn the multiply knob, the answer gets multiplied by the input temperature. A big input like 212 makes the computer turn that knob way too hard, and the learning blows up. Shrinking the inputs to small numbers keeps both knobs moving at a sensible pace. This is called normalization, and nearly every real neural network does it. (The knob values in this page are converted back to normal degrees so they're easier to read.)

9From one neuron to AI chatbots

Our network has 2 knobs. The neural networks that recognize faces, translate languages or power AI chatbots work the same basic way, just much bigger:

  • Instead of 1 neuron, they have millions, connected in layers where the outputs of one layer feed into the next.
  • Instead of 2 knobs, they can have billions.
  • Instead of 7 examples, they learn from millions or billions of them.
  • Each neuron also adds a small twist (called an activation function) so the network can learn curvy, complicated patterns, not just straight lines.

But the learning loop is the same one you just watched: guess, score the mistakes, find downhill, turn the knobs a little, repeat. If you understand our tiny neuron, you understand the core idea.

10Words to know

Neuron
A tiny machine that takes numbers in, multiplies and adds, and puts a number out.
Neural network
One or more neurons that learn from examples. Ours has just one.
Weight (w, multiply knob)
How much the input gets multiplied by.
Bias (b, add knob)
A number added to every guess, which slides the line up or down.
Training data
The examples with known right answers that the network learns from.
Loss (mistake score)
One number that says how wrong the guesses are. Lower is better.
Gradient (slope)
Which way, and how strongly, turning a knob changes the score.
Gradient descent
Learning by taking small steps downhill on the mistake score.
Learning rate (step size)
How big each knob adjustment is.
Epoch (round)
One pass through all the examples, followed by one adjustment.
Normalization
Shrinking the input numbers so learning stays steady.
Test data
New examples the network never trained on, used to check that it really learned.
